# Playbooks by stage, and the craft

## The hire thesis

Before a word of the resume, write one to three sentences naming why this
reader should call this person, in the reader's terms. The page argues the
thesis; what does not serve it goes.

- Club: "Ran two events end to end as a freshman; has the design skills the
  marketing committee lacks; shows up."
- VP Engineering: "Scaled an org from 12 to 90 through two funding rounds while
  shipping the platform behind $40M ARR; has done exactly this in the reader's
  sector."

## Stage playbooks

### Student applying to a club, program, scholarship, or campus job

- Reader: a student officer or a program coordinator with 40 applications and
  ten minutes. What sells: initiative (things started, organized, fixed), fit
  (they can picture you in the role), reliability.
- Order: Header → Education (school, major, expected year; GPA if 3.5+ or
  asked; coursework only when it carries the thesis) → Leadership & Activities
  → Projects → Experience (any job counts: shifts, tutoring, retail — that is
  reliability and scope) → Skills.
- High school: first- and second-years may keep two or three high-school lines
  that carry the thesis (the club, the award, the job). Drop them after that.
- Length: one page with generous white space. Two or three bullets per item.
- Interview: "What did you start, organize, or fix that nobody asked you to?"
  "How many people, how much money, how often?" "What would the club get from
  you in the first month?"
- Before → after:
  - "Member of robotics club" → "Built the drivetrain for a 6-person FRC team
    that placed 4th of 38 at the regional; taught the CAD workflow to 5 new
    members."
  - "Worked at a coffee shop" → "Ran the closing shift solo 3 nights a week
    for 14 months; trained 4 new hires; zero cash discrepancies."

### Internship or first job

- Reader: a campus recruiter or a hiring manager screening 200 applicants. What
  sells: evidence you can do the entry-level job (projects, coursework applied,
  prior internships), speed of learning, the vocabulary of the field.
- Order: Header → Education → Experience (internships, research, part-time) and
  Projects, whichever is stronger first → Leadership → Skills.
- Length: one page. No summary. Skills: the tools and languages the posting
  names that you have actually used.
- Interview: "For each project: what did it do, who used it, what was hard,
  what was the result?" "Which posting keywords have you touched, even briefly,
  in a course or project?"
- Before → after: "Data analysis intern; helped with reports" → "Automated the
  weekly sales report in Python and SQL, cutting a 6-hour manual process to 20
  minutes for a 12-person team."

### Early career (1–4 years)

- Reader: a hiring manager. What sells: results in the first roles, scope
  growth, a promotion or expanded ownership.
- Order: Header (headline = target title) → Experience → Projects if they carry
  the thesis → Education (short) → Skills.
- Length: one page. Summary optional, two lines, only if the target title
  differs from the current title.
- Interview: "Compared to when you started, what do you own that you did not
  own before?" "What did your manager write in your last review?"

### Mid-career (5–12 years)

- Reader: a hiring manager or recruiter comparing five finalists. What sells:
  outcomes at scale, ownership, cross-functional leadership, a coherent arc
  toward the target.
- Order: Header (headline) → Summary (two or three lines: target title, years,
  two proof points) → Experience (recent roles four or five bullets, older ones
  one to three) → Skills → Education (one line per degree).
- Length: one page; two if the second is full of results.
- Interview: "The three outcomes from the last five years you would want in
  the first line?" "Scope: team, budget, users, revenue?" "What did you change
  that outlived you?"
- Before → after: "Managed the onboarding project" → "Cut time-to-first-value
  from 3 days to 40 minutes by replacing a 9-step setup wizard with templates,
  lifting trial conversion from 9.4% to 12.3% across 41,000 trials."

### Senior and executive

- Reader: a recruiter or board member who reads the top half, then the scope
  line of every role. What sells: scale (P&L, headcount, growth),
  transformations delivered, judgment, and selectivity — what you left out
  signals seniority.
- Order: Header → Executive summary (three lines) → Selected achievements (four
  or five bullets, the career's greatest hits with numbers) → Experience (every
  role: one scope line such as "P&L $120M, 340 people, 6 countries", then three
  or four outcome bullets; roles older than 15 years compressed into one
  "Earlier career" paragraph) → Board & advisory → Education → Publications and
  talks only if they carry the thesis.
- Length: two pages. Never three.
- Dates: month and year on roles; the graduation year is optional past 15
  years.
- Interview: "Which results would the reader check with a reference?" "What was
  the business before and after you?"

### Career change

- Reader: someone who must be convinced the transfer is real. What sells: a
  headline and summary that name the target role and bridge from the old one;
  experience regrouped so the transferable results come first; the vocabulary
  of the new field; proof of commitment (a credential, a project, a volunteer
  role in the new field).
- Order: Header (headline = target role) → Summary (the bridge in three lines)
  → Relevant experience (roles or projects that prove the target skills,
  regardless of date) → Additional experience (the rest, one line each) →
  Education & credentials → Skills.
- Never a functional resume (skills with no chronology); readers assume it
  hides something. The chronology stays visible under Additional experience.
- Interview: "Which parts of the old job were the new job in disguise?" "What
  have you already done in the new field, paid or not?"

### Returning after a gap

Use the playbook of the stage before the gap. A gap over six months gets one
honest entry: `### Career break | Caregiving, relocation, or study | 2022 –
2024`, with one bullet if anything relevant happened (a course, a volunteer
role, freelance work). Lead with the strongest pre-gap role; keep dates on
everything.

### Academic and research

A CV, not a resume: no length limit, no summary. Order: Education →
Appointments → Publications (reverse chronological, full citations,
first-author marked) → Grants and awards → Teaching → Talks → Service →
Skills. Use the classic theme, and A4 outside North America.

## Bullet craft

1. **XYZ.** Accomplished [X] as measured by [Y], by doing [Z]. When the number
   is the headline, lead with it: "Lifted retention 18% by …".
2. **Verb first, past tense** (present tense for the current role). No
   pronouns, phrases not sentences.
3. **One idea per bullet, two lines max.** A third line is a second bullet or
   a cut.
4. **Numbers whenever true**: %, $, count, time, rank, scale, frequency.
   Without a number, use scope ("for a 40-store region"), frequency ("weekly
   for two years"), comparison ("first in the team's history"), or who noticed
   ("cited in the CEO's all-hands").
5. **Result before method** when the result is strong; method first when the
   method is the skill being sold.
6. **Specific beats impressive.** "Redesigned checkout" beats "Drove
   transformational e-commerce initiatives".
7. **Cut the weak openers**: responsible for, helped, assisted with, worked
   on, participated in, tasked with, duties included. The renderer flags them.
8. **Recent roles get the most bullets** (four or five); old roles get one or
   two, or an "Earlier career" line.
9. **Mirror the posting's nouns** inside these bullets when the claim is true.

### Action verbs by what the bullet proves

- Built or created: built, designed, developed, engineered, launched, shipped,
  founded, established, authored, produced, prototyped, architected
- Led or organized: led, ran, directed, coordinated, chaired, mobilized,
  recruited, hired, trained, mentored, coached
- Improved or fixed: improved, cut, reduced, accelerated, streamlined,
  automated, consolidated, simplified, resolved, stabilized, migrated
- Grew or won: grew, expanded, increased, doubled, won, closed, secured,
  raised, generated, sold, negotiated, landed
- Analyzed or decided: analyzed, modeled, forecast, measured, evaluated,
  audited, diagnosed, prioritized, tested, researched, identified
- Communicated or taught: presented, wrote, published, taught, explained,
  documented, translated, briefed, advised, persuaded

Vary them. Never start two adjacent bullets with the same verb.

## The summary (mid-career and above, career change)

Three lines: **[Target title] with [N] years [doing what, for whom]. [Proof
point with a number]. [Second proof point, or the angle that matters to this
reader].** No adjectives about character. No "seeking a position".

## The keyword pass (after the draft, before the render)

1. List the posting's required nouns: titles, tools, methods, certifications,
   domains.
2. For each: is it in a bullet or in Skills? If the person can defend it, add
   it where it is true. If not, leave it out and tell the person it is a gap
   the cover letter or the interview must address.
3. Use the posting's spelling and capitalization ("PostgreSQL" when they write
   it that way; "Kubernetes (K8s)" when both forms appear).
4. The headline carries the target title when the current title differs.

## The look

Personality on a resume is voice, selection, and a restrained typographic
identity. It is never graphics, icons, columns, or skill bars. Map the Look
answer to a theme and an accent, then let the field's norms override:

| They want to read as | Theme       | Accent             |
| -------------------- | ----------- | ------------------ |
| Conservative         | `classic`   | `black` or `navy`  |
| Clean                | `modern`    | `navy`, `charcoal` |
| Warm                 | `warm`      | `rust`, `plum`     |
| Bold                 | `editorial` | `plum`, `forest`   |
| Technical            | `technical` | `teal`, `charcoal` |

Field norms that win over the pick: law, finance, consulting, government,
medicine, and academia read `classic`; a two-page senior resume reads
`compact` or `classic`; a student club application can carry `warm` or
`editorial` when the person wants it. One accent per resume, and the name is
the only large thing on the page. If they hesitate, point them at the Look menu and the accent picker in the
preview tab: whatever they pick lands in the front matter of `resume.md`.

## Header and contact

- Name as they want to be called, the headline, then: City, ST · phone · email
  · linkedin.com/in/custom · portfolio or GitHub when the target reads code or
  design.
- One phone, one email, a professional address. No street address, no photo
  unless the region calls for it, no birth date.
- Links as plain visible text (linkedin.com/in/name). They must survive print
  and parsing.

## What to leave off

References line · objective statement · high school past the second year of
college · "Microsoft Office" · hobbies that do not carry the thesis (for a club
application they often do) · the pronoun "I" · salary · reasons for leaving ·
every course taken · logos, icons, skill bars, columns.

## Proofreading pass (last, every time)

Read the PDF's text bottom to top. Check: one date format throughout; month
and year on every role; consistent tense; no double spaces; no `[needs number]`
markers; the file named `Firstname-Lastname-Resume.pdf`; links resolve; the
phone carries a country code when applying abroad.
